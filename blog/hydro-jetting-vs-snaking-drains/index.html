    Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Drain Cleaning Method Do You Need?

    By Aaron Mitchell · August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

    Know the Clog First

    Not every clog is the same, and the tool should match what is actually in the pipe. A wad of hair in a tub drain is a different job from grease coating 60 feet of kitchen line. Clogs fall into two groups, a single plug at one spot or buildup along the whole pipe wall, and figuring out which one you have is the first thing a good tech does. Usually that means a few questions.

    Scale is the slow version of buildup. Old cast iron drains collect scale and rust that close the opening a little every year. A 4-inch cast iron line with heavy scale might have the flow area of a 2-inch pipe, which is why it clogs again a week after a cable goes through it. A snake punches through without removing it.

    Much of the water in Mississippi comes from underground aquifers and is fairly soft, though some supplies carry iron or a sulfur smell.

    How a Drain Snake Works

    A snake, or drain auger, is a coiled steel cable spun by a hand crank or a motor. The tip carries a cutting head or a spring that bores into the clog and either breaks it up or hooks it. Hand augers handle sinks and tubs, a closet auger handles toilets, a small drum machine handles laundry and kitchen lines, and a sectional machine with a 3/4-inch cable reaches a main line out to 100 feet or more. Snaking is quick and costs less.

    Snaking is the right first call for a single hard blockage. Cables do well on a toy in a toilet or a root ball at one joint in the main. The weakness is that a cable opens a hole roughly the size of its cutter, so grease and scale on the rest of the pipe wall stay right where they were. Repeat clogs are the tell.

    Cable size matters as much as the machine. A 1/4-inch hand cable is right for a bathroom sink but useless on a 4-inch main. Running too big a cable through a small trap can crack it, and running a small cable through a main just pokes a hole through a root mass that grows back within weeks. Picking the right cable is half the job.

    How Hydro Jetting Works

    A jetter pushes water through a hose at roughly 3,000 to 4,000 psi for residential lines. Its nozzle fires one jet forward to break through and a ring of rear jets that drive the hose and scrub the pipe. As the tech reels the hose back, the rear jets strip grease, scale, sludge and roots off the pipe all the way around, and the debris flushes downstream to the sewer. What is left is a nearly clean pipe wall.

    Jetting shines on greasy kitchen lines and scaled cast iron. Restaurants have their lines jetted on a schedule for exactly this reason. It also clears sand and silt that a cable just pushes around, and a jetted line usually stays clear far longer than a cabled one, often years instead of months on a grease problem. The visit itself costs more.

    Jetting needs a way in. An exterior cleanout is the best access point, since it keeps the mess out of the house. Homes without one may need a cleanout installed first, usually a quick job with a fitting and a riser at the foundation, and that new access point pays for itself the next time the line needs work. A toilet flange can serve as access when nothing else will.

    Choosing Between Them

    Jetting is not safe for every pipe. Badly rotted cast iron or broken clay can come apart under high pressure. That is why a good tech runs a camera first on an older line, both to see what the clog is and to check whether the pipe can take a jetter without making the problem worse. Without the camera, a cleaning can end up as an excavation.

    Here is the rough rule we use. One hard plug in pipe that is in decent shape gets snaked. A kitchen line that clogs every few months or a main coated with grease or scale gets a camera and then a jetter, and that combination usually stops the repeat calls for a long time. Cost plays into it too.

    When to Call a Professional

    Rental drum machines are where a lot of homeowner drain jobs go sideways. A cable that kinks inside the pipe can get stuck or whip around and hurt someone. If a plunger and a small hand auger have not cleared it, or more than one drain is backing up, call DFM Plumbing at (601) 265-9260 and describe which fixtures are affected, because that tells the tech which line to start on and which tools to bring. Mention when the last cleaning was.
