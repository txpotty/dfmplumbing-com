    Grease Trap Replacement and Upgrades in Southaven

    Plastic and steel traps both wear out, just in different ways. Steel rusts at the seams and the baffle welds. Plastic cracks. We remove the old unit, set the new one in the same spot when it fits, and use no-hub couplings so future work is easier.

    Large Outdoor Grease Interceptor Installation in Southaven

    Outdoor gravity interceptors run from 750 gallons to well past 2,000, and setting one means an excavator on site and a traffic-rated lid if it sits in the drive lane. Before anything ships, we check that the tank and covers carry an H-20 traffic rating where trucks will roll over them. Most go in over two to four days.     Restaurant Grease Trap Compliance in Southaven, MS

    Most sewer authorities run a FOG program with an inspector who opens your trap and checks your pump-out manifests against the schedule they set. A failed inspection can bring a fine or an order to upgrade. We install to pass. We add a sampling port if they require it and size to the authority's own formula.

    Hydro-Mechanical Grease Trap Installation in Southaven, MS

    Hydromechanical traps use baffles and a vented flow control to pull grease out of water moving at a set rate, usually 20 to 100 gallons per minute. You can put one under a sink, beside a dish station or in a pit in the slab. The flow control is not optional. Without it, a dumped sink pushes water through faster than the unit is rated for and the grease goes out the outlet. We look for units certified to PDI G-101 or ASME A112.14.3.

    Grease Trap Sizing and Placement in Southaven, MS

    Sizing starts with fixture drain rates and ends with where the trap can physically go. A trap 40 feet from the sink lets grease cool and stick in the pipe before it ever reaches the unit. Closer is better. We also check that a pump truck can reach an outdoor tank with about 100 feet of hose, since a tank the hauler cannot reach costs you more on every service.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Southaven

    Expect roughly $1,500 to $4,000 for a small indoor trap and $7,000 to $20,000 or more for an outdoor gravity interceptor. A floor-recessed trap costs more than one set on the floor. Location drives the rest. Lids under traffic cost more, and so does repaving.

    Pump-outs every couple of weeks on a trap that used to go two months is the clearest sign you have outgrown it or the baffles have failed. Grease odor in the dining room is another. Rust shows up too. We also look downstream, because grease in the outlet pipe means the trap is letting it through.

    It keeps cooking grease out of the public sewer. Hot greasy water cools as it moves through the trap, the grease floats to the top, and cleaner water leaves through the outlet. Without one, that grease hardens in the city mains and causes sewer overflows, and sewer authorities fine the kitchens they trace it to. Your own drain lines benefit too.

    Coverage comes from two places. The manufacturer warrants the trap body and lid, and the term depends on the brand and whether it is steel or plastic. We put our labor warranty in writing on the invoice, covering leaks at the joints we made and any problem with how we set and leveled the unit. Damage from skipped cleanings falls outside it.

    Half a day for most indoor units. That covers setting the trap, piping the inlet and outlet, installing the flow control and running a water test. Gravity interceptors take two to five days on site, plus time for the inspector to see the tank before backfill and again at final. Permits add lead time.

    Nearly every food business needs one. Codes require one wherever sinks or dish machines send greasy waste to the sewer. A few low-grease operations get waived, and the sewer authority makes that call. Ask before you sign the lease.

    The common rule is to clean before grease and solids fill 25 percent of the trap's depth. Busy kitchens with small indoor traps often hit that mark every week. Outdoor tanks go longer. A gravity interceptor typically gets pumped monthly to quarterly, and some sewer programs set the schedule for you.

    In everyday talk the two names get swapped. Strictly, a grease trap is the small indoor unit rated in gallons per minute, and an interceptor is the large tank rated in gallons of capacity. Codes vary on the wording. Some plumbing codes call both of them interceptors and separate them as hydromechanical and gravity, so we use whatever terms your permit office uses.
